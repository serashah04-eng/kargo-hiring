KARGO HIRING RUBRIC: PRODUCT MANAGER & SENIOR PRODUCT MANAGER
Built from 8 past-hire CVs and the two Kargo JDs (PM, SPM)

IMPORTANT LIMITATION (Step 1)
The uploaded files contain no performance ratings, outcomes, or interview notes.
The .docx files were checked, including comments, metadata, and footnotes, and none
are present. As agreed, the hires were NOT sorted into High / Meets / Below tiers.
The patterns below are behaviours that recur across MULTIPLE past-hire CVs, backed by
concrete, verifiable claims in those CVs. They are NOT confirmed to predict high
performance at Kargo. Once ratings are available, re-check each pattern: keep it only
if it shows up more in high-rated hires than in lower-rated ones.

Past hires reviewed: Rohan Desai (engineering, ex-CHA ops), Sunita Krishnamurthy (ops /
compliance consultant), Vikram Nair (PM, HR tech), Aditya Shetty (enterprise sales,
logistics SaaS), Preetham Rao (backend engineering, e-comm), Meghna Tiwari (customer
success, ex-freight), Lavanya Iyer (PM, port/logistics SaaS), Rahul Bose (B2B marketing).

Patterns found but DROPPED because the JDs already cover them:
- Building team standards and processes that others adopt (Vikram, Meghna, Sunita,
  Rohan). PM JD: "The rhythms a PM function needs." SPM JD: "Helping define the
  practices... the PM function will run on."
- Ground-level operations experience (Rohan, Sunita, Meghna, Lavanya, Aditya). PM JD:
  "Genuine curiosity about how operations work at ground level." SPM JD: familiarity
  "is a genuine advantage."
- Learning from killed or failed work (Lavanya, Aditya). PM JD: "shipped things, killed
  things, and learned from both."
These still appear in the rubrics where the evidence supports them, but they are not
counted as hidden patterns.

## 2. Product Manager Rubric

Scoring: rate each criterion 1–5, where 1 = no evidence, 3 = some relevant evidence, and
5 = strong evidence matching the right-hand column. Weighted score = sum of
(rating ÷ 5 × weight). Probe each criterion with a "tell me about a specific time"
question, and score only concrete, checkable examples.

| Criterion | Definition (incl. why it matters at Kargo) | Evidence | What Strong Looks Like | Weight |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------- | ---------------------- | ------ |
| 1. Unprompted build → peer adoption (P1) | Spots an unowned operational pain, builds a rough working fix without being asked, and gets it adopted. WHY: Kargo has no PM function, handbook or template, so nobody will hand this person problems. It also directly tests the JD's 6-month goal of features customers use "without being asked to." | Rohan (B/L prototype → 30 users in 1 month; Excel tracker → 12 users in 2 weeks), Lavanya (dashboard adopted by 2 other teams), Preetham (unrequested dashboard found a ₹12L/month bug), Sunita (weekend workflow rebuild kept permanently) | Names a specific pain they noticed themselves, what they built and how fast, and who adopted it without a mandate, with numbers. Weak: every example was an assigned project. | 25% |
| 2. Lived operations fluency | Has personally done or sat inside freight, customs, carrier or port work, not just interviewed people who do. WHY: the PM owns the tool ops teams "live inside," and the JD's 6-month goal is time "in the rooms where the work actually happens." | Rohan (180+ shipments/month of documentation at a CHA), Sunita (200+ shipments/month, DGFT and customs work), Meghna (overnight customs-hold resolution), Lavanya (carrier allocation, 800+ shipments/month), Aditya (worked alongside terminal ops during peak periods) | Can walk through a real freight forwarder's day: documents, holds, DO releases, berth windows, and what breaks. Can name a workflow gap they saw from inside the operation. | 20% |
| 3. Ship–kill–document discipline | Kills work based on usage data, even when discovery was positive, and writes up why things failed so the lesson spreads. WHY: the JD asks for short cycles of shipping and killing. The evidence adds that the strongest examples produced a written artifact that others reused. | Lavanya (killed 2 features despite strong qualitative demand, moved capacity to a workflow with 3x weekly active use, wrote internal and customer-facing outage post-mortems); Aditya (post-mortem on a lost deal became standard qualification practice) | Gives a real kill decision with the data behind it and what replaced it, and has a written post-mortem that changed how the team works. Weak: only success stories, or "we learned a lot" with no artifact. | 20% |
| 4. Silent recovery under pressure (P2) | When something breaks, fixes it fast and keeps the customer from absorbing the pain. WHY: customers run daily operations on Kargo, and the first PM will often be the person closest to a live failure. | Meghna (overnight customs hold resolved before the client knew), Sunita (weekend workflow rebuild after an unannounced vendor change), Rohan (same-day fix for a peak-hour outage) | A concrete incident with timeline, what they personally did, and customer impact (ideally none). Weak: escalated and waited, or the story is only about the team. | 20% |
| 5. Builds rhythms others adopt | Creates templates, frameworks or processes that become the team standard. WHY: the JD makes this core to the first PM role. The evidence shows the key signal is adoption by others, not just creation. | Vikram (PRD template and sprint review adopted by the 4-PM team), Meghna (30-60-90 framework adopted by the full CS team), Sunita (SOPs adopted in full within 60 days) | Shows the artifact, who adopted it, and what measurably changed, for example less misalignment or fewer queries. | 15% |

**Total Weight: 100%** (25 + 20 + 20 + 20 + 15)


## 3. Senior Product Manager Rubric

Scoring: same 1–5 method as the PM rubric. The SPM rubric gives more weight to
integration and migration judgment, decision ownership, and growing people, because the
SPM JD centres on the integration and data layer, consequential calls, and a possible
Head of Product path.

| Criterion | Definition (incl. why it matters at Kargo) | Evidence | What Strong Looks Like | Weight |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------- | ---------------------- | ------ |
| 1. Integration & migration judgment under live stakes | Has moved or connected live operational systems (vendors, carriers, 3PLs, FMS) without disrupting users, and made the build / configure / avoid call. WHY: the SPM owns carrier, port and ERP integrations, with consequences "two years from now." | Rohan (led a data-vendor migration with no data loss and 60% less lag), Preetham (3PL integrations with Delhivery, Bluedart and Ecom Express; batch-to-event migration cutting lag from 4 hours to 8 minutes; zero-downtime index change), Sunita (scoped and ran a paper-to-cloud FMS migration, including vendor management and go-live), Lavanya (API docs cut integration time by 2 weeks) | A named migration or integration with its trade-offs, the rollback or safety plan, measured result, and one thing they deliberately chose not to build. | 25% |
| 2. Silent recovery & reliability instinct (P2) | Treats failures as their own responsibility, fixes fast, and shields customers. WHY: the JD asks for data quality freight forwarders "can stake their operations on." The evidence shows personal behaviour in incidents, not just standards on paper. | Rohan (40% fewer P1 incidents after introducing on-call and code review), Preetham (22-minute average P1 resolution; ₹12L/month bug fixed in 48 hours), Meghna (overnight customs fix), Sunita (no disruption despite an unannounced vendor change) | A specific incident plus a structural fix that stopped it happening again, such as on-call, monitoring or review. | 20% |
| 3. Documented decision ownership | Makes calls in ambiguity, commits, and writes down the reasoning, including when they were wrong. WHY: "no committee approves product decisions," and the roadmap must not change "three times in six weeks." | Lavanya (kill decisions based on usage data; engineering lead said she "doesn't hedge"; owned outage follow-ups to closure), Aditya (written root cause on a lost deal adopted as standard) | A hard call made without senior cover, its written rationale, what happened, and what they would change. Referees confirm the team trusted the call. | 20% |
| 4. Grows people to promotion (P3) | Develops others with measurable outcomes. WHY: the SPM is the most senior PM and a possible Head of Product. Neither JD tests this, but 4 past hires show it. | Rohan (mentee promoted within 14 months), Sunita (trainee promoted to team lead within 18 months), Preetham (2 mentees, structured 1:1s), Rahul (hired and onboarded a 4-person team) | Names the people they developed, what they did differently for each, and the outcome, such as a promotion or a new scope. Weak: "I mentor informally." | 15% |
| 5. Lived operations fluency | Has direct, hands-on experience in logistics or operations-heavy work. WHY: the JD calls this "a genuine advantage, not a nice-to-have." Integration choices depend on how forwarders actually work. | Rohan, Sunita, Meghna, Lavanya, Aditya (see PM criterion 2) | Can explain how a real integration gap, such as a slot booking or document handoff, breaks a forwarder's workflow. | 10% |
| 6. Commercial read of product gaps | Understands why deals stall, such as the buyer, the procurement cycle, or a missing integration, and connects product work to revenue. WHY: the 6-month goal is an integration that "unblocked a stalled deal." | Aditya (lost-deal root cause: misread the IT procurement cycle and the wrong economic buyer; case study programme), Lavanya (API docs shortened customer integration), Rahul (case studies became the most-used sales collateral) | Names a specific deal or segment a product or integration change unlocked, and how they found the blocker. | 10% |

**Total Weight: 100%** (25 + 20 + 20 + 15 + 10 + 10)
